Title Insurance 101

What is title insurance — and why do I need it?

When you take out a mortgage, one part of your closing costs will be title insurance. The premium is a one-time charge, and the policy protects the lender. You can also purchase owner's title insurance to protect yourself, though it's not required.

Title insurance is a policy that covers third-party claims on a property that don't show up in the initial title search and arise after a real estate closing. A third party is someone other than the property's owner — such as a construction company that wasn't paid for its work under a previous owner. The term "title" refers to someone's legal ownership of the property.

A title claim could arise at any time, even after you've owned the property with no problems for many years. Someone else might have ownership rights you don't know about when you make an offer. Even the current owner might not be aware that someone else has a claim — in the case of an overlooked heir, even the person with those rights might not know they have them.

Before your home loan closes, your mortgage lender will order a title search from a title company. The title company searches public records — deeds, mortgages, divorce decrees, court judgments, tax records and child support orders — to find any defects that could affect the lender's or buyer's property rights. If the search reveals problems (also called "clouds"), the title company works to resolve them.

Liens

Placed by a contractor, tax authority, or lender who hasn't been paid. You don't want to get stuck paying a previous owner's unpaid bills.

Easements

Someone else's right to use your property even though you own it — for example, utility lines in your backyard — which could limit how you use it.

Encumbrances

Includes liens and easements, plus zoning laws, restrictive covenants imposed by HOAs, and leaseholder rights.

Coverage

What title insurance covers — and what it doesn't.

A policy covers underlying issues with a property's title that might have been missed before you bought — handy if the public-record search failed to catch a lien or ownership dispute.

What it covers

  • Property survey errors & boundary disputes
  • Errors on the property deed
  • Building code violations by a previous owner
  • Conflicting wills & claims by an ex-spouse who didn't sign off
  • Forged & improperly recorded documents
  • Liens from contractors, taxing entities or previous lenders
  • Encroachments

What it doesn't cover

Title insurance doesn't protect against problems caused by your own actions after you buy the property, such as:

  • Failing to pay the company that replaced your roof
  • Failing to pay your property taxes
  • Eminent domain — a government seizing property for public use

In short, it protects against issues that might have affected your decision to purchase had you known about them at the time — not issues newly created after you buy.

Standard vs. expanded coverage

Homeowners can choose between standard and expanded coverage, which broadens protection against certain additional risks. We'll help you see exactly what expanded coverage adds and decide what's right for your situation.

Cost & coverage

Who pays for title insurance — and how much?

An owner's policy can cover paying off a previously undiscovered lien, defending a lawsuit over a claim to the property, or providing a cash settlement to a buyer who unwittingly purchased with a forged deed — and it protects your ability to sell the home later.

A one-time cost

Title insurance is a one-time, up-front fee — not an ongoing expense. An owner's policy is based on the home's purchase price; a lender's policy on the loan amount.

Typical price

Both policies together usually cost about 0.5%–1.0% of the purchase price — roughly $1,500 to $3,000 on a $300,000 home, according to ALTA.

Same price in NC & SC

In North and South Carolina, the price is the same no matter which title company you use — so choose a reputable company with experienced underwriters.

Who pays for title insurance?

The buyer pays for the lender's policy as part of their closing costs. Either the buyer or seller can pay for the owner's policy on behalf of the buyer — local real estate custom often determines who pays. Buying an owner's policy at the same time as a lender's policy can reduce the owner's-policy cost through a "simultaneous issue charge."
